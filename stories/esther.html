Esther: The Queen Who Saved Her People

Esther 1–10

A young Jewish woman became queen of a powerful kingdom. When her people faced a terrible danger, she had to decide whether she would remain silent or risk everything to help them.

A King Without a Queen

Long ago, in the Persian Empire, there was a king named Ahasuerus, also known as Xerxes. He ruled over a vast kingdom stretching across many lands.

During a great royal banquet, the king commanded Queen Vashti to appear before his guests. She refused, and the king became angry. After seeking advice from his officials, he removed Vashti from her position as queen.

Later, the king's servants began searching the kingdom for a new queen. Among the young women brought to the royal palace was a Jewish woman named Esther.

Esther's Secret

Esther had been raised by her cousin Mordecai after her parents died. Mordecai cared deeply for her and had guided her throughout her life.

Esther was beautiful and graceful, and she found favour with those who cared for the women in the palace. Eventually, she was chosen to become the new queen.

Mordecai had advised Esther not to reveal that she was Jewish. She followed his advice and kept her background private.

Even after becoming queen, Esther remained connected to Mordecai. He regularly waited near the palace gates to learn how she was doing.

Haman's Dangerous Pride

A man named Haman was promoted by the king to a position of great authority. The king commanded his officials to bow before Haman, but Mordecai refused.

Mordecai would not bow to him, and Haman became furious. Instead of punishing Mordecai alone, Haman decided to destroy all the Jewish people throughout the Persian Empire.

Haman convinced the king to issue a royal decree ordering the destruction of the Jews on a chosen date. The decree was sent throughout the kingdom, causing fear and grief among the Jewish people.

Mordecai's Message

When Mordecai learned about the decree, he tore his clothes, put on sackcloth and ashes, and mourned publicly.

Esther heard that Mordecai was distressed and sent a messenger to discover what had happened. Mordecai explained Haman's plan and asked Esther to go before the king and plead for her people.

Esther was frightened. Persian law stated that anyone who approached the king without being summoned could be put to death unless the king extended his golden sceptre.

Esther had not been called to see the king for thirty days. Approaching him was a dangerous decision.

For Such a Time as This

Mordecai sent Esther an important message. He reminded her that being queen might have placed her in a position to help her people.

He asked her to consider whether she had become queen for such a time as this.

Esther realised that she could not remain silent while her people were in danger. She asked Mordecai to gather the Jewish people in Susa and have them fast for three days. She and her attendants would fast too.

Then Esther made her decision: she would go to the king, even if it meant risking her life.

Esther Approaches the King

On the third day, Esther put on her royal robes and stood in the inner courtyard of the palace, where the king could see her.

When the king noticed her, he extended his golden sceptre. Esther approached and touched its tip.

The king asked what she wanted and promised to grant her request, even offering her up to half his kingdom.

Esther did not reveal everything immediately. Instead, she invited the king and Haman to a banquet she had prepared.

At the first banquet, she invited them to another meal the following day. Haman was pleased to receive such attention, but his anger towards Mordecai continued.

The King's Sleepless Night

That night, the king could not sleep. He ordered that the royal records be read aloud to him.

During the reading, he discovered that Mordecai had once uncovered a plot against his life. Mordecai had saved the king, but had never received a reward.

The next morning, Haman arrived at the palace intending to ask the king for permission to execute Mordecai.

Before Haman could make his request, the king asked him how someone whom the king wished to honour should be rewarded.

Thinking the king meant him, Haman suggested that the person should be dressed in royal clothing, placed on the king's horse, and led through the city by a noble official.

The king ordered Haman to do exactly that for Mordecai.

Haman was forced to honour the very man he had wanted to destroy.

Esther Reveals the Truth

At the second banquet, the king again asked Esther what she wanted.

This time, Esther told him that she and her people had been condemned to destruction. She explained that Haman was responsible for the terrible plan.

The king was furious. Haman was punished for his actions, and the king gave Esther and Mordecai authority to issue a new decree.

Since Persian royal decrees could not simply be cancelled, a new decree allowed the Jewish people to defend themselves against those who attacked them.

When the appointed day arrived, the Jewish people were able to protect themselves. Their enemies were defeated, and the danger that had threatened them was overcome.

The Festival of Purim

The Jewish people celebrated their deliverance with joy and feasting. Mordecai established an annual celebration called Purim, named after the lots Haman had cast to choose the day of destruction.

To this day, Purim is celebrated by Jewish communities around the world. It remembers Esther's courage, Mordecai's faithfulness, and the deliverance of the Jewish people.

Esther had once been a young woman living quietly in the palace. But when the moment came, she used her position and her voice to protect an entire people.

What Can We Learn?

  • Courage means doing what is right even when we are afraid.
  • We can use our positions and abilities to help others.
  • Speaking up can make a difference, even when it is difficult.
  • We should not allow pride or jealousy to control our actions.
  • Faith, patience, and wisdom can guide us through difficult situations.
  • One person's brave decision can affect the lives of many people.

Think About It!

Esther knew that approaching the king could cost her life, but she chose to help her people. Can you think of a time when doing the right thing might feel frightening? What could help you find the courage to do it?
